Pecking Order Lab

Six agents with hidden, unequal abilities repeatedly pair off and play a coordination game: each round, either one backs down and lets the other lead, both back down at once, or neither does and a costly conflict breaks out. Nobody is optimizing — each agent follows a fixed, psychologically "hardwired" rule for forming expectations about who outranks whom, based on what they've personally experienced and what they've watched happen to others. The paper's central finding: these rules reliably stop conflict, but they only sometimes build a hierarchy that actually tracks ability — there's a tradeoff between sorting quickly and sorting correctly. Adjust the rules on the left and run the group to see where a given rule set lands on that tradeoff.

Sirianni, A. D. (2018). "Expectations and Coordination in Small Groups." Advances in Group Processes, Vol. 35, pp. 181–207. · DOI · source notebook

Three kinds of dial drive every run. Game sets the stakes of a single interaction — how many agents, how many rounds, what's at risk when nobody backs down. Rule is the hardwired automaton every agent uses to decide how to act and update its expectations. Ability controls how talent is spread across the group and how strongly it converts into reward, independent of anyone's behavior.
